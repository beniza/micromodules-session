Building micromodules with AI

Micromodule Practice Pack

Short, hands-on practice so you leave today with prompts you can reuse to plan, write and illustrate a micromodule with AI.

Before we start

2 minutes

Important: never paste SIL content or anyone's personal data into a personal AI account. Everything on this page uses made-up practice topics, so it's safe to use.

2

Three rules from how AI works

Each one leads to a simple habit.

  • It predicts likely words. So it sounds sure, even when it's wrong. Habit: check facts against your source.
  • It only knows what's in this chat. It can't see your notes, your learners or your goals unless you tell it. Habit: give it your source content and context.
  • It forgets between chats. A new chat starts from zero. Habit: keep your instructions in one reusable prompt.

Practice 1: Prompt makeover

About 5 minutes

Here's a weak prompt. It will get you something, but probably something generic.

Weak prompt
Write a micromodule about giving feedback.

Your turn: rewrite it in Gemini using the 5 ingredients below. Then compare the two answers.

  • RoleWho should the AI act as?
  • AudienceWho is it for, and what do they already know?
  • Source and contextYour notes, facts and situation.
  • Task and formatWhat to make, and in what shape.
  • ConstraintsLength, tone, and your quality bar.
Show a strong example

Same topic, all 5 ingredients. The notes are invented, so you can run it as it is.

Strong prompt
ROLE: You are an experienced learning designer who writes short, practical workplace training.

AUDIENCE: New team leaders who have never given formal feedback before. They are busy and will read this on a phone between meetings.

SOURCE: Use only these notes:
- Give feedback soon after the event.
- Describe what you saw, not the person.
- Explain the impact it had.
- Ask for their view, then listen.
- Agree one next step together.

TASK AND FORMAT: Write a 5-minute micromodule as 6 web cards: 1 title card, 4 content cards (one idea each) and 1 card with a 3-question quick check.

CONSTRAINTS: Plain, warm language. Bullets of 4 to 5 words that start with a verb. No jargon. If my notes don't cover something you need, write NEEDS SOURCE instead of inventing it. Before you write, ask me up to 3 questions if anything is unclear.

Practice 2: Let the AI interview you

About 5 minutes

This is called meta prompting: asking the AI to help you write the prompt. Fill the highlighted [slots] with a practice topic, such as "running effective meetings" for "new team leaders".

Option A: Interview me
I want to create a 5-minute micromodule on [topic] for [audience]. Before writing anything, interview me one question at a time to understand the audience, goals, tone and format. After my answers, write the best possible prompt for this task, then wait.
Option B: Improve my prompt
Improve this prompt: [paste your prompt here]. Tell me what's missing, then rewrite it.

Why it works: the AI knows what makes a prompt good, and you know what you need. The interview brings the two together.

5

Practice 3: The image surprise

About 6 minutes

You've just seen some flat illustrations. Now try this prompt in Gemini and see what comes back.

Image prompt 1
Create an image: a photorealistic photo of a team leader giving feedback to a colleague at a small table in a bright modern office, natural light, shallow depth of field, 35mm. The people are invented, not anyone real.

Now try the same scene with a different style. Only the underlined words change.

Image prompt 2
Create an image: a team leader giving feedback to a colleague at a small table in a bright modern office, as a flat vector illustration, warm neutral palette, simple shapes, no text. The people are invented, not anyone real.

Talk about it

  • Which image would you use in a module, and why?
  • What could go wrong with realistic people?
After you've tried it

A few words control the whole style. "Photorealistic, 35mm, shallow depth of field" asks for a photo; "flat vector, simple shapes" asks for an illustration. Name the style you want, every time.

Realistic people bring extra risks in learning content:

  • They change from image to image. The "same" person rarely looks the same twice, which breaks a story.
  • They can misrepresent cultures. Clothing, settings and faces may be stereotyped or simply wrong.
  • They may look like real people. That can mislead learners or cause real harm.
  • Text inside images often comes out garbled. Add text yourself, outside the image.

A simple, consistent illustration style is usually safer, kinder and easier to keep consistent.

Your micromodule assistant

Take this home

Paste this as the first message in a new chat (Gemini or Claude). It turns the chat into a co-creator that interviews you, works only from your content, and checks with you at every step.

Starter prompt
You are my micromodule co-creator. A micromodule is a short piece of learning (about 3 to 7 minutes) on one topic, delivered as an infographic, a set of web cards, a short podcast or a short video. Follow these rules for this whole conversation.

HOW WE WORK
1. Interview me first. Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer before asking the next. Find out: who the audience is; the one thing they should be able to do afterwards (the goal); the format (infographic, web cards, podcast or video); the length; and the tone. Then summarise what you heard in 5 short bullets and ask me to confirm.
2. Ask me for my source content (notes, a draft, key facts). Work only from what I give you. If something is needed but is not in my source, write "NEEDS SOURCE" followed by what is missing, instead of inventing it.
3. Propose before you produce. Work in small steps: (a) outline, (b) text, (c) visuals. At each step, show me your proposal and wait for my approval or changes before moving on. Never skip ahead.

TEXT RULES
- One idea per screen, card or scene.
- Bullets are 4 to 5 words and start with a verb.
- Use plain language. If you must use a technical term, explain it in one line.

VISUAL RULES
- At the start of the visuals step, agree one visual style with me, then use that same style for every image.
- If there is a character, describe them once in detail and repeat that exact description in every image prompt, so they look the same each time.
- Avoid text inside images unless I ask for it; text is added separately.
- Never use the real names of people, and never show real or famous people. Use roles (for example "the team leader") or clearly invented first names.

AT THE END OF EVERY OUTPUT
Add a short "Human review" checklist: facts to check against my source, any NEEDS SOURCE gaps, decisions I need to approve, and anything to test (for example, how it looks on a phone).

Start now by asking your first interview question.

Save it as a Gem (a Gem is a saved assistant in Gemini that remembers its instructions).

Look for Gems in the Gemini menu, create a new Gem, and paste the prompt above into its instructions. Then start any new micromodule from that Gem.

Take it further

A tool for each format

  • Gemini: draft text and create images, all in one chat.

  • ElevenLabs: give a script you've written a natural-sounding voice.
  • Claude: build interactive web pages, cards and video or podcast scripts.
